<div class="answer-box">Most traffic challans need no lawyer at all — a compoundable offence is paid online or at a Lok Adalat. A lawyer or free legal aid matters only once a challan is contested or is non-compoundable and goes before a magistrate.</div>

Before looking for representation, check which kind of challan you actually have. [Court challan vs on-spot challan](/court-challan-vs-on-spot-challan/) explains the split: a compoundable offence carries a fixed amount you can pay on the e-challan portal or at [a National Lok Adalat](/e-challan-lok-adalat/) with no court appearance at all, while a non-compoundable one (chiefly drunken driving and most of dangerous driving) or one you choose to contest on [vcourts.gov.in](/virtual-court-challan/) goes before a magistrate, where representation can matter.

## Who is entitled to a free lawyer for a case like this?

Section 12 of the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 lists who "shall be entitled to legal services under this Act" for any case they have to file or defend. The categories, verbatim from the Act: a member of a Scheduled Caste or Scheduled Tribe; a victim of trafficking or begar; a woman or a child; a person with disability; a person in circumstances of undeserved want such as a mass disaster, ethnic violence, caste atrocity, flood, drought, earthquake or industrial disaster; an industrial workman; a person in custody; or a person whose annual income is below the limit the state government (for cases below the Supreme Court) or the Central Government (for Supreme Court cases) prescribes. The Act itself still names the 1987 floor figures of ₹9,000 and ₹12,000, but Section 12(h) lets the government prescribe a higher ceiling by rule — check your State Legal Services Authority's current figure rather than relying on those numbers. Nothing in Section 12 excludes a traffic prosecution; the entitlement turns on who you are and what you earn, not what you are charged with, though under Section 13(1) the authority still has to be satisfied you have a genuine case to prosecute or defend.

## How do I actually apply?

NALSA's legal-aid page lists several routes, none of which require you to already have a lawyer:

- **In person or by post** at a Taluk Legal Services Committee, your District Legal Services Authority (DLSA), the State Legal Services Authority, a High Court Legal Services Committee, or the Supreme Court Legal Services Committee — whichever has jurisdiction over your case. Each keeps a front office where you submit a ready-made form, or a plain written application stating your name, gender, address, employment, nationality, SC/ST status, monthly income, the type of case, and why you need aid.
- **Online** through the Legal Aid Application on [scourtapp.nic.in](https://scourtapp.nic.in), through nalsa.gov.in, or through your State Legal Services Authority's own site.
- **By email** to nalsa-dla@nic.in, or **orally** to a Para Legal Volunteer or an authority officer, who can fill in the form for you if you cannot read or write — you then sign or give a thumbprint.

Bring identity proof, any certificate or affidavit proving your Section 12 category, and an affidavit stating your income. NALSA's page says the application is scrutinised by the Member-Secretary or Secretary and a decision is generally due within 7 days. If granted, a panel lawyer is assigned and the institution — not you — pays the drafting, process and lawyer's fees.

## What if I don't qualify for free legal aid?

Then it is an ordinary engagement: you hire and pay a private advocate yourself. Section 12 decides who gets legal services for free, not who may be represented — anyone can engage an advocate for a contested or court challan regardless of income. Confirm any advocate you engage is currently enrolled by checking with the State Bar Council for the state where they practise; this site does not run a lawyer directory and makes no referral.

## Do I need a lawyer at all if I'm just paying online?

No. Paying a compoundable challan on the e-challan portal, through [how to pay an e-challan online](/how-to-pay-e-challan-online/), or settling at a Lok Adalat closes the case the moment payment clears — there is no hearing to be represented at. Representation becomes relevant only once you choose "contest" on a Virtual Court case or face a non-compoundable charge that was never payable in the first place. This is general information, not legal advice. For your specific case, consult an advocate.
